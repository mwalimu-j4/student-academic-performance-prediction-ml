# Results and Discussion

## Results

The approved early-prediction experiment used the verified UCI Mathematics dataset. The target was `pass = 1` when `G3 >= 10` and `fail = 0` otherwise; `G1`, `G2`, and `G3` were excluded from the predictors. A stratified 80/20 split with random seed 42 produced 316 training examples and 79 held-out test examples. The test set contained 26 fail and 53 pass examples. Preprocessing was fitted inside each model pipeline using the training data only, and five-fold stratified cross-validation was performed on the training set. On the held-out test set, the Decision Tree achieved accuracy 0.5949, pass precision 0.7234, pass recall 0.6415, pass F1 0.6800, and ROC-AUC 0.5287. Its fail-class precision was 0.4063, recall was 0.5000, and F1 was 0.4483. The Random Forest achieved accuracy 0.7089, pass precision 0.7679, pass recall 0.8113, pass F1 0.7890, and ROC-AUC 0.6295; its fail-class precision was 0.5652, recall was 0.5000, and F1 was 0.5306. The corresponding confusion matrices, ordered as fail then pass, were `[[13, 13], [19, 34]]` for the Decision Tree and `[[13, 13], [10, 43]]` for the Random Forest. Cross-validation on the training set showed the same general pattern: Random Forest mean F1 was 0.7703 and mean ROC-AUC was 0.6795, compared with 0.6971 and 0.5717 for the Decision Tree.

## Discussion

The model-selection criterion was stated before interpreting the results: prefer higher held-out recall for the fail class when identifying students at risk of failing, with fail-class F1 as the tie-breaker. Both models had fail recall of 0.5000, so neither model identified more of the failing students in this particular test split. The Random Forest is nevertheless the more appropriate model under the stated tie-breaker and supporting evidence because its fail F1 was 0.5306 versus 0.4483, its fail precision was 0.5652 versus 0.4063, and it also had higher accuracy, pass F1, and ROC-AUC. This comparison supports selecting the Random Forest over the Decision Tree baseline, but it does not establish a universal performance threshold: no external target threshold was specified, and both models missed 13 of the 26 failing students in the held-out set. The project objectives of implementing a reproducible, leakage-controlled comparison and reporting executed evaluation results were met; the evidence does not support claiming that the model is sufficiently accurate for high-stakes academic decisions.

Important limitations affect interpretation. The dataset contains 395 examples from two Portuguese secondary schools, and the held-out test set contains only 26 fail examples, so individual predictions can substantially change the reported rates. Pass is the majority class (265 of 395 examples), making accuracy alone inadequate; the report therefore includes class-specific metrics, F1, and ROC-AUC. The original school and questionnaire context may not represent other institutions, countries, cohorts, or time periods. The fixed holdout and internal cross-validation provide useful evidence but not independent external validation. Future work should test on a larger and more diverse cohort, use institution- or time-based external validation, tune the decision threshold using training folds only when the cost of missed failing students is defined, assess probability calibration and subgroup performance, and evaluate whether additional pre-period information is available without violating the intended prediction point.

## Summary and Conclusion

The executed evidence indicates that the Random Forest is preferable to the Decision Tree for this early student pass/fail task: it achieved held-out accuracy 0.7089, pass F1 0.7890, ROC-AUC 0.6295, and higher fail precision and F1, while matching the Decision Tree's fail recall at 0.5000. The project met its reproducibility, leakage-control, model-comparison, and reporting objectives, as confirmed by the clean pipeline run and seven passing automated tests, but neither model can be considered a dependable standalone method for identifying every student at risk because each missed half of the failing test examples and the data are small and context-specific. The Random Forest should therefore be treated as a research result and candidate for further validation, not as an automated basis for academic decisions.
